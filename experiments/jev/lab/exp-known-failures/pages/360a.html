Is Disney Over-Relying on Ryan Murphy?

Dana Walden needs new TV hits, and happily welcomed the star showrunner back after his Netflix run. But while Murphy’s generated no shortage of buzz (Love Story, All’s Fair, and his latest, The Shards), it translates less often to Nielsen magic.

Showrunner Ryan Murphy Photo: JC Olivera / Variety via Getty Images
Matthew Belloni
August 7, 2026

Did you know Ryan Murphy hasn’t had a show chart on Nielsen since he’s been back at Disney? So declared a recent lunch companion, referring to the weekly top 10 streaming charts of the biggest hits with U.S. audiences. I had assumed that the prolific showrunner’s ripped-from-the-tabloids miniseries Love Story: John F. Kennedy Jr. & Carolyn Bessett would have charted, given all the media hoopla. Or maybe All’s Fair, last fall’s Kim Kardashian spectacle of wigs and costume design that generated historically bad reviews but tons of social buzz. The Shards, a trashy Bret Easton Ellis collaboration featuring Murphy’s signature blend of porny-looking hot people and gruesome murder, will surely chart after its well-marketed debut last night, right?

But my agent friend was correct. The Murphy oeuvre has made 35 total appearances all-time in the Nielsen streaming top 10, but the only series to chart since he returned to making new shows for Disney after five years at Netflix, in the summer of 2023, were installments of Monster—Murphy’s serial killer exploitation anthology… for Netflix. There are some caveats: Love Story, like many Murphy shows, also appeared on the FX linear channel and was thus considered an “acquired” title—meaning it needed to generate more viewing minutes than “originals” to appear on a chart dominated by shows with hundreds of episodes. (Nielsen counts viewing for all episodes of a show.) But HBO’s biggest series often make the cut, and Murphy has been considered among TV’s most reliable hitmakers for nearly two decades. He’s a brand unto himself, and is paid as such. He should be there.

Yet when Nielsen released its top 20 shows of the first half of 2026 this week, neither Love Story nor his other recent offerings, All’s Fair and the similarly pilloried The Beauty, made the list. Taylor Sheridan and Shonda Rhimes, the two writer-producers to whom Murphy is often compared, each had a show there. As did Dan Fogelman, another big TV producer signed to a comparable deal at Disney (though Murphy is far more likely to achieve the productivity bonuses that each has, per two sources).

So what’s up? Ryan Murphy has hardly become ratings poison, of course. And despite his outsize profile (more on that below), Murphy’s brand of provocative and often R-rated mayhem and smack-talking has always been hit-and-miss with general audiences. I asked Nielsen for total viewership numbers for recent Murphy shows, including both linear and streaming, and they revealed that he still can deliver above-average ratings.

But those numbers are… fine. Doctor Odyssey, which was also panned by critics, got a boost from airing on ABC before getting canceled after one season. Turns out Love Story, which Murphy didn’t write, did generate hit-level viewership, so your mom was right when she told you her friends were all watching. Additionally, Disney says the show delivered 5.4 billion minutes streamed worldwide and became one of the top D+ subscriber-acquisition drivers globally this year. Murphy’s also got franchises at Disney that aren’t included here, like the still-chugging American Horror Story and the over-the-top rescue procedural 911, which will air two versions this year.

But… a bunch of stinkers in there too. I’d forgotten about the lame sitcom Mid-Century Modern and the Halloween horror play Grotesquerie. And even those more-popular shows aren’t hits on par with the bigger shows on Netflix or Prime Video. Both Paradise and Alien: Earth were far bigger on Hulu than Love Story or the Murphy output. Given his massive deal, that carefully curated aura around him, and the decades-long reliance on his output by Disney’s newly promoted content chief, Dana Walden, some might assume the Murphy shows were performing better. I certainly did. So it’s worth asking: Is Disney relying too much on Ryan Murphy?

Axe to the Slate

That’s a particularly relevant question given the challenges facing Walden in streaming, and especially her original TV series strategy. Disney C.E.O. Josh D’Amaro revealed stronger financials on Wednesday in the direct-to-consumer business, with revenue up 11 percent, to $5.53 billion, in the latest quarter, and operating income (profits) more than doubling, to $712 million, compared to the quarter prior. But as Bloomberg noted last week, those streaming gains are largely due to price hikes, improvements to the tech stack and user interface, and better bundling of the Disney services—not Walden’s group producing more popular shows.

In fact, as my colleague Julia Alexander noted yesterday, Disney+/Hulu still leans more than any other streamer on old, library series with hundreds of episodes, like Grey’s Anatomy and Family Guy, as well as the kids’ stuff like Bluey. The newer offerings? Not so much. Despite a regular slate of originals from Hulu, FX, and Disney+, including shows based on well-known properties like Percy Jackson and Marvel heroes—I.P. that Netflix would kill or, worse, suck up to Trump’s Justice Department, to own—most of the original TV output doesn’t rate at the same level as the hits on rival platforms. In the first half of 2026, Disney placed only one show in the top 20 original series list (Paradise, at No. 14), fewer than Peacock and the same as—gulp—Apple TV.

The popularity of ESPN sports, library shows, and all those blockbuster movies on Disney+ often saves Disney when it comes to time spent on its platforms. But while its share of total TV viewing has risen from the mid 4 percent range in 2023 to around 5 percent lately, streaming viewership has been stagnant for a couple years now, according to Nielsen.

Maybe that’s because Disney, under Walden and film chief Alan Bergman, who oversees Marvel and branded TV output, has taken an axe to the overall slate. They’re not alone in town, of course. We’ve all seen that chart showing how overall scripted and unscripted series in production has cratered by a third since the peak of Peak TV in 2022. But a decent chunk of that decline is due to cutbacks by Walden’s group. She, Bergman, Disney TV Group president Craig Erwich, and the others reduced the number of shows on Disney platforms by almost 50 percent between 2022 and 2025, according to Ampere Analysis as cited by Bloomberg. Just last week, Disney un-renewed the Marvel series Wonder Man despite renewing it in March. Series co-creator Destin Daniel Cretton, who just directed Marvel’s Spider-Man: Brand New Day, the biggest movie of the year, pointed his finger not at Marvel’s Kevin Feige but at the Disney higher-ups: “I know this sounds weird—but if it were up to Kevin, we would be doing that show.”

Walden said last week that she feels “great about what we’ve accomplished” in streaming, but Disney has basically slashed costs and raised prices to get to profitability. Doesn’t mean there aren’t still a bunch of hits, especially by the company’s standards. High Potential and The Rookie on ABC, Shogun and The Bear on FX/Hulu—and, yes, Murphy’s Love Story—all deliver. But after nearly seven years of Disney+ and almost three years since the company’s deal to take full control of Hulu, the services are still largely used to catch up on linear hits and watch old movies and shows. There’s no platform-defining smash, à la Landman on Paramount+ or Reacher on Prime Video or Love Island on Peacock. Disney stopped reporting subscriber numbers, so we don’t even know if the platforms are growing. Walden clearly knows this is an issue. She said last week that she’s planning to increase the content spend, especially internationally, “while significantly expanding our creative output over the next several years.”

Which Brings Us Back to Murphy…

Despite the cuts to its overall content output, Disney has continued to invest freely in the Murphy conveyor belt of shows, even renewing All’s Fair for a second season despite U.S. numbers on par with The Beauty, which was canceled. (Maybe Kardashian’s Hulu reality show had something to do with that.) And Walden, who has been close friends with Murphy for decades and is godmother to his children, continues to talk up their partnership both internally and externally, despite most of his recent shows not breaking through.

You probably didn’t know any of the ratings numbers above because Murphy, his publicist, Simon Halls, and the Disney P.R. apparatus have been relentless in building up his aura of invincibility. When All’s Fair premiered to some of the worst reviews in years—sample headline, from Buzzfeed: “All’s Fair on Hulu: Is It That Bad?”—Disney responded with a press release declaring the show “Hulu Originals’ Biggest Scripted Series Premiere in Three Years.” There will probably be a similarly amusing announcement for The Shards on Monday.

It’s all part of the perception game. Back when Murphy, a former Entertainment Weekly journalist, first rose to prominence with Nip/Tuck and Glee, he was a pioneer of sorts in making himself famous.  As the Hollywood press coverage migrated online and “TV showrunners” became household names, he leaned in, sometimes doing multiple interviews a week and positioning himself as a cultural kingmaker, able to win his talent fandoms and Emmys. I never really dealt with him much in my years at Hollywood Reporter, though I always kinda admired that he wasn’t afraid to be interesting in the press, saying controversial things or posing with his nude 1-year-old son on a THR cover. (Yes, that happened.)

But I heard plenty of stories of toxicity around Murphy, both from people who worked with him and others in the media—especially about how mean and vindictive he and Halls could be. It was considered a badge of honor for journalists if Murphy called, screamed, and hung up. Fox and 20th TV execs were trained to know that press requests for any star on a Murphy show must first be approved by Halls, who made Murphy and his collaborators available only to favored outlets that covered Murphy’s character musings and plot teases as breaking news. Conversely, they ignored and undermined anyone perceived as insufficiently fawning. The message was clear: Cross TV’s biggest showrunner and you were cut off… or worse.

Murphy, now 60, is said to have matured and mellowed in recent years. (He declined to comment.) He picks fewer fights and is nicer to collaborators, including at this week’s roller rink promotion for The Shards. He’s back working with Glee co-creator Brad Falchuk after a long estrangement. Those in Ryan’s close orbit remain incredibly loyal, and the head of his production company, Alexis Martin Woodall, has spent her entire career with him. He’s no longer the same sort of Emmy magnet—the seven noms for Love Story is, again, fine, but far from when The People vs. O.J. Simpson collected 22 noms and nine wins a decade ago. And the last thing stars like Glenn Close or Naomi Watts want to be asked about is the horrible critical response to All’s Fair—though Brad Simpson, one of Murphy’s producing partners on a half-dozen shows, is taking on a bigger role to improve Season 2.

So if I’m Walden, I’m probably playing the long game with Murphy, enduring the ratings disappointments and critical drubbings for the hope of another Horror Story or 911. After all, his five years at Netflix were marked by some instantly forgettable flops (Hollywood, Halston) and then Monster, one of the platform’s biggest franchises. But at what cost? Disney needs big original hits now. Murphy is a high-priced slugger who takes a lot of swings, but at this point, he’s not hitting many home runs.
